**UrjaDrishti: AI-Powered Renewable Generation Forecasting for Karnataka**

*A Presentation-Ready System Explanation*

---

**The Problem We Are Solving**

Karnataka's electricity grid is undergoing a fundamental transformation. With over 2,050 megawatts of solar capacity concentrated at the Pavagada Solar Park — the largest operational solar park in Asia — and significant wind generation flowing through the Gadag corridor in the northern part of the state, the grid now derives more than fifty percent of its power from renewable sources on favorable days. This is a remarkable achievement. It is also, operationally, one of the most difficult problems grid operators face anywhere in the world.

The challenge is not simply that solar and wind power are variable. The challenge is that they are variable in ways that are hard to predict precisely. A cloud front moving across the Deccan Plateau can reduce Pavagada's output by thirty percent in under two hours. A shift in the southwest monsoon wind pattern can bring Gadag's turbines to cut-out speed unexpectedly. Without accurate, timely, and uncertainty-aware forecasts of what each plant will produce, operators are forced to hold large reserves of expensive backup generation — spinning standby capacity that burns fuel and costs money even when it is never dispatched — purely as insurance against forecast error. At Karnataka's scale, a fifteen percent forecast error at the Pavagada cluster translates directly into roughly three hundred megawatts of unnecessary reserve allocation. This has both economic and environmental consequences.

What operators need, and what this system provides, is not just a single predicted number for tomorrow's generation. They need to know how confident that prediction is. They need to understand, in plain language, what is driving the forecast up or down. They need the forecast to update automatically as the day progresses and real measurements come in. And they need all of this to work without modifying the existing SCADA infrastructure, without sending sensitive data to any cloud service, and without requiring months of historical data per plant before the system can be trusted.

---

**System Architecture and Data Flow**

UrjaDrishti is architected as a layered forecasting system that sits entirely on top of existing infrastructure. Data flows in one direction: SCADA outputs are read passively, the forecasting engine processes them, and predictions are served to operator dashboards through a REST API. Nothing writes back to legacy systems. This is a deliberate design constraint that ensures zero operational risk during deployment.

The backend is implemented in Python using FastAPI, which provides asynchronous, high-performance routing with automatic documentation generation. All API routes are protected by API key authentication, rate-limited using SlowAPI, and fully logged to an audit trail. The frontend is a React application built with Vite, styled with Tailwind CSS, and visualized using Recharts for the probabilistic forecast ribbons.

The data pipeline originates with a synthetic data generator that produces one full year of hourly generation data across six plants representing two geographically distinct clusters: Cluster A covering the Pavagada Solar zone with three solar plants of 150, 120, and 90 megawatts respectively, and Cluster B covering the Gadag Wind corridor with three wind plants of 100, 80, and 60 megawatts. Because real SCADA data cannot be shared for training purposes under Karnataka's data governance requirements, the system uses a Gaussian Copula synthesizer from the SDV library to generate physically realistic correlated weather and generation data. This is not a compromise — it is the correct approach. A Gaussian Copula preserves the statistical dependence structure between variables: the fact that high irradiance tends to coincide with low cloud cover, that temperature correlates with season, that wind speed autocorrelates over time. Synthetic data generated this way faithfully captures the distributions and cross-variable relationships that a model needs to generalize well.

---

**Data Engineering and Physics-Informed Feature Transformation**

Before any machine learning model ever sees the data, two critical physics-based transformations are applied. This is arguably the most important architectural decision in the entire system, and it is what separates this approach from naive statistical forecasting.

For solar plants, the raw Global Horizontal Irradiance is never fed directly to the model. Instead, the system first computes what the irradiance would be under perfectly clear sky conditions at that location, time, and date, using the Ineichen-Perez clear sky model implemented through the pvlib library, with Linke turbidity values calibrated monthly to Karnataka's atmospheric conditions — ranging from 3.0 in the dry winter months to 4.5 during the monsoon peak. It then divides actual GHI by the clear sky GHI to produce the Cloud Modification Factor, or CMF. CMF is bounded between zero and one: a value of one means the sky is perfectly clear and all theoretically available radiation is reaching the panel; a value of zero means total overcast. This normalization is powerful because a CMF of 0.7 in December and a CMF of 0.7 in June represent the same physical state — seventy percent of potential irradiance — even though the raw irradiance values differ enormously between those two months. A model trained on CMF generalizes across seasons and geographies in a way that a model trained on raw GHI cannot.

For wind plants, an analogous transformation applies. Raw wind speed is passed through a tabulated turbine power curve based on the Suzlon S111 2.1 megawatt turbine, which is commonly deployed across Karnataka's wind farms. This lookup uses numpy interpolation between manufacturer-specified data points covering the full range from the 3 meters-per-second cut-in speed, through the cubic ramp region, to the 16 meters-per-second rated speed where full output is achieved, and finally to the 25 meters-per-second cut-out speed where the turbine shuts down for safety. By converting wind speed into a power curve fraction — a dimensionless number between zero and one — the system explicitly encodes the non-linear, threshold-driven physics of turbine operation before the model trains. The model then only needs to learn residuals around this physics baseline.

Beyond these primary features, the system engineers several supporting inputs. Time is encoded cyclically using sine-cosine pairs for both hour of day and day of year, ensuring that the model perceives midnight and one in the morning as adjacent points rather than the extremes of a linear scale. Geographic coordinates are similarly encoded as sine-cosine transformations of latitude and longitude, preserving spatial proximity relationships. Asset metadata — installed capacity, tilt angle for solar panels, hub height for turbines — is included as features so that a single global model can distinguish between plants without needing separate models for each. Finally, a simulated NWP ensemble spread is computed for each hour by generating ten perturbed variants of the base weather signal and measuring their standard deviation. On clear stable days, this spread is small; before monsoon fronts or wind ramps, it is large. This spread becomes a direct input feature that allows the uncertainty estimation layer to widen prediction intervals automatically when atmospheric conditions are more chaotic.

**The Forecasting Model: A Global Two-Stage Architecture**

The core forecasting model is a LightGBM gradient boosting regressor trained globally across all six plants simultaneously. This is a deliberate departure from the more intuitive approach of training one model per plant. The global architecture offers two critical advantages. First, it requires only that asset characteristics be available as input features rather than requiring months of historical generation data per plant — this means a new plant can be onboarded on day one simply by encoding its physical parameters, with uncertainty intervals widening appropriately until sufficient historical data accumulates to calibrate them. Second, the global model can transfer patterns learned from solar plants to improve predictions at other solar plants with different capacities or locations, as long as their physical characteristics are described in the feature set.

The feature set entering the model comprises seventeen columns: the two primary physics features (CMF and power curve fraction), temperature, NWP ensemble spread, capacity, the four geographic encoding dimensions, tilt angle, hub height, the four temporal encoding dimensions, a season indicator, and plant type encoded as a binary integer. LightGBM receives this feature matrix and produces a point forecast for each plant-hour combination.

The stage-two residual corrector is what enables meaningful intra-day updates. As the day progresses and real SCADA readings become available, the system computes the difference between what Stage One predicted and what actually occurred for each elapsed hour. These residuals are summarized as a mean and standard deviation over the most recent six hours, capturing both the direction and consistency of any systematic bias. The Stage Two model — also a LightGBM regressor — takes these recent error statistics alongside the hour of day, the current physics features, and the asset metadata, and predicts how much the Stage One forecast should be corrected for each remaining hour of the day. Applying this correction shifts the entire prediction interval rigidly, preserving the interval width while re-centering the distribution on the corrected point estimate. The practical effect is that by noon, when six hours of actuals are available, the afternoon forecast is measurably more accurate than the pure day-ahead prediction — and this improvement is quantified in the evaluation framework.

---

**Uncertainty Quantification with Conformalized Quantile Regression**

The uncertainty layer is built on Conformalized Quantile Regression using the MAPIE library. Rather than training a single model that produces a point estimate, the system trains three separate LightGBM quantile regressors targeting the tenth, fiftieth, and ninetieth percentiles of the generation distribution. These raw quantile predictions are then calibrated using a dedicated holdout calibration set — three months of data that the base models never saw during training.

MAPIE's conformalization step computes nonconformity scores on the calibration set and adjusts the prediction intervals until they achieve the target coverage property: the stated eighty percent confidence interval must contain the actual value approximately eighty percent of the time. This is not a heuristic approximation; it is a mathematically guaranteed coverage property that holds by construction for any new data drawn from the same distribution. The system further enforces monotonicity by clipping cases where the raw P10 estimate exceeds the P50 or the P90 falls below the P50, which can occasionally occur when independent quantile models disagree at extreme conditions.

The NWP ensemble spread feature plays a particularly important role here. Because spread is an explicit input to all three quantile models, the prediction intervals adapt automatically to atmospheric uncertainty: on a clear stable day, the P10-to-P90 gap narrows because the model has seen that low-spread conditions correspond to predictable outcomes; on a monsoon cloud-ramp day, the same model produces wide intervals because high spread reliably predicts high forecast variance. This adaptive behavior is exactly what grid operators need — not fixed confidence bands, but confidence bands that communicate the actual difficulty of the current forecast situation.

---

**SHAP-Based Explainability**

Every forecast is accompanied by plain language alerts generated from SHAP values. SHAP, or SHapley Additive Explanations, uses the TreeExplainer algorithm to compute, for each input feature, how much that feature pushed the model's prediction above or below the expected baseline for that plant. The system extracts the top three drivers by absolute SHAP magnitude for each hour and maps them through a template system that converts technical signals into operator-facing language. A large negative SHAP value on the CMF feature becomes the message that heavy cloud cover is the primary driver of reduced generation. A large positive SHAP value on the power curve fraction becomes confirmation that wind speed is approaching rated threshold and strong output is expected. Eight or more distinct alert patterns are implemented, covering cloud cover suppression, clear sky boost, wind ramp events, temperature-driven efficiency loss, peak solar hour positioning, high uncertainty flags, and seasonal effects.

This explainability layer is crucial for operator trust. When a forecast changes significantly between the day-ahead and the noon intra-day update, operators need to understand why before they adjust their dispatch schedules. An unexplained number change creates confusion and distrust; a plain language explanation of what changed in the atmosphere restores confidence in the system.

---

**Hierarchical Reconciliation**

Plant-level forecasts and cluster-level forecasts are generated independently, which means they will not be numerically consistent by default — the sum of individual plant predictions may not match the cluster total. This inconsistency, if left uncorrected, creates contradictory dashboards: a plant engineer sees one set of numbers while a cluster dispatcher sees another, for the same time period. This destroys trust in the system entirely.

The solution is Minimum Trace, or MinT, reconciliation. This is a post-hoc matrix operation that simultaneously adjusts both plant-level and cluster-level forecasts to be numerically coherent at every level of the hierarchy, while minimizing the total adjustment in the trace of the covariance matrix. The result is that plant forecasts sum exactly to cluster totals, and cluster totals aggregate correctly to the system level. MinT requires no retraining, no architectural changes, and can be applied as a final post-processing step on any set of independently produced forecasts.

**Evaluation Framework**

The evaluation harness is built around a strict rolling temporal holdout: the final two months of data form the test set, the two months before that form the validation set, three months before that constitute a dedicated calibration set for MAPIE, and everything prior is training data. This structure prevents any future information from contaminating training windows, which would artificially inflate apparent performance.

Three baselines are implemented for comparison. The persistence baseline forecasts tomorrow's generation as identical to today's actuals from twenty-four hours prior — this is the simplest possible forecast and represents the minimum bar a model must clear to be useful. The climatological mean baseline computes the average historical generation for each plant at each hour of each month from the training set and uses these averages as forecasts — this captures diurnal and seasonal patterns but nothing about today's weather. The raw NWP linear regression baseline trains a separate linear model per plant using raw weather variables without any physics transforms or asset encoding — this deliberately naive baseline is included specifically to isolate the value contributed by the physics transforms and the global architecture.

The primary evaluation metric is CRPS, the Continuous Ranked Probability Score, which jointly rewards accurate point estimates and well-calibrated uncertainty intervals. A model that is confident but wrong is penalized more severely than one that honestly expresses uncertainty. Supporting metrics include normalized Mean Absolute Error and normalized Root Mean Square Error, both normalized by plant capacity to allow comparison across plants of different sizes. Coverage verification confirms that the eighty percent prediction interval actually contains the true value approximately eighty percent of the time across all test samples.

Stress tests exercise four edge-case scenarios generated by the data pipeline: a sudden cloud ramp where CMF drops from 0.9 to 0.15 over two hours on an otherwise clear afternoon, a ten-day monsoon onset simulation with progressively deteriorating irradiance, a wind speed spike approaching and crossing the cut-out threshold, and a sustained week-long deep irradiance suppression. On these scenarios, the model's point forecast accuracy degrades — as it should, because these are genuinely difficult atmospheric conditions — but the prediction intervals widen appropriately, maintaining calibration and communicating to operators that the forecast should not be trusted for tight scheduling.

**System Design Constraints and Deployment Architecture**

The system is designed from the ground up to satisfy five non-negotiable constraints for government deployment. First, no existing SCADA system is modified — the forecasting layer reads only and writes to nothing. Second, all computation occurs on-premise within KREDL-KSPDCL premises, with no data leaving Karnataka's administrative perimeter. Third, no hosted LLM is used anywhere in the production path; the explainability is generated through SHAP values mapped through a deterministic template system, with an offline quantized Small Language Model specified as the production upgrade path. Fourth, the system operates entirely on synthetic data for training, validated through the Train on Synthetic, Test on Real framework. Fifth, all API routes are rate-limited, authenticated, and logged for full audit traceability.

The security model is layered. API key authentication prevents unauthorized access to forecast endpoints. SlowAPI enforces rate limits of thirty requests per minute on forecasting routes and sixty on evaluation routes, protecting against denial-of-service conditions. Full HTTP request logging with client IP addresses creates an audit trail. TLS termination at the nginx reverse proxy layer encrypts all traffic in transit. Alert delivery through Twilio WhatsApp and SendGrid email is implemented as a notification layer on top of the core forecasting function, not as a dependency.

**Robustness Considerations**

Karnataka's four seasons — winter, summer, monsoon, and post-monsoon — are handled explicitly through the seasonal encoding feature and through the Linke turbidity calibration values that vary month by month. The monsoon's dramatic effect on solar irradiance, where cloud cover can suppress generation by fifty to sixty percent for weeks at a time, is captured both in the synthetic data generation process and in the NWP spread simulation, which produces higher ensemble spread during monsoon months. The frontend dashboard applies scenario-specific modifications to prediction band widths when a stress scenario is selected, making the increased uncertainty visually apparent to operators.

Missing data is handled at multiple levels. The physics transforms return well-defined values for all weather inputs within physical bounds, and the data validation pipeline checks for NaN values across all twenty-one feature columns before any model training or inference proceeds. The dashboard's service layer implements mock fallbacks for every API call, ensuring that the operator interface remains fully functional — with clearly labeled cached data — even if the backend becomes temporarily unreachable. This is not cosmetic; it is essential for a system that grid operators will depend on during exactly the situations — cloud ramps, storm events — when the backend may experience elevated load.

Geographic variation across the six plants is handled through the asset encoding strategy. Different latitudes and longitudes encode different solar irradiance basins; different altitudes affect both clear sky modeling and wind power law scaling. The hub height correction for wind plants, which scales ten-meter NASA POWER wind measurements to hub height using the Hellmann power law exponent appropriate for open terrain, ensures that wind speed inputs reflect the actual conditions experienced by the turbine rather than the surface measurement.

**Strengths of the Solution**

Several architectural choices distinguish this system from generic machine learning approaches to renewable forecasting. The physics-first feature engineering — CMF and power curve fraction replacing raw irradiance and wind speed — means the model learns weather-to-generation residuals rather than the full non-linear mapping from scratch, which both improves accuracy and dramatically reduces the data required for convergence. The global architecture means new plants onboard immediately without historical data requirements, which is critical in a state like Karnataka where renewable capacity is expanding continuously. The guaranteed coverage property of Conformalized Quantile Regression means the uncertainty bands are statistically honest rather than heuristically constructed, which supports confident scheduling decisions. The plain language explainability means operators understand not just what the system predicts but why, which is essential for adoption in an operational control room environment.

The two-stage intra-day correction framework is particularly powerful because it separates two distinct sources of forecast improvement: the day-ahead model captures structural patterns from physics features and historical data, while the Stage Two corrector captures systematic bias specific to today's atmospheric conditions as revealed by the morning's actual measurements. Together they produce afternoon forecasts that are meaningfully more accurate than either stage alone.

**Honest Assessment of Limitations and Future Work**

The current system's most significant limitation is the use of synthetic training data. While the Gaussian Copula preserves statistical relationships and the physics transforms enforce physical plausibility, synthetic data cannot capture all the plant-specific operational idiosyncrasies — inverter clipping behavior, specific soiling patterns, maintenance schedules — that real SCADA data would reveal. The Train on Synthetic, Test on Real validation framework is the correct approach, but the practical accuracy improvement on real data relative to synthetic data will depend on how faithfully the synthetic distribution matches the true operational distribution.

The forecasting architecture also uses LightGBM, which, while highly effective and computationally efficient, does not natively model spatial dependencies between plants. A cloud front that passes over Chitradurga before reaching Pavagada creates a predictable temporal lag in irradiance suppression that a graph-based model like a Spatio-Temporal Graph Neural Network could exploit explicitly. The production roadmap specifies STGNN as the upgrade path once six months of real operational data have accumulated. The transition requires only replacing the Stage One model; all surrounding architecture — the CQR calibration layer, the Stage Two corrector, the reconciliation framework, the dashboard, and the API contracts — remain unchanged.

Similarly, the explainability layer currently uses template-based text generation rather than a generative language model. This produces grammatically consistent but lexically constrained alerts. The offline quantized Small Language Model specified for production deployment would enable more nuanced, context-sensitive explanations, including alerts that reference multiple interacting factors and provide more specific operational recommendations.

In summary, UrjaDrishti represents a complete, deployable, physically grounded forecasting system that is designed not as a research prototype but as Phase One of a production infrastructure — capable of delivering immediate operational value while remaining architecturally ready to incorporate more sophisticated modeling components as operational data and computational resources allow.
