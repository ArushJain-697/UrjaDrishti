The Problem We Are Solving
Karnataka has solar plants and wind plants spread across different geographies. Each plant produces electricity every hour. The grid operator needs to know, before the day starts, how much each plant will generate in each of the next 24 hours. That is the day-ahead forecast.
But weather changes during the day. By noon, you have the actual morning readings. If the forecast was wrong in the morning, you want to use that information to fix the afternoon forecast. That is the intra-day update.
Stage 1 handles the day-ahead forecast. Stage 2 handles the intra-day correction.

What "predicting for a particular plant" means
Every row in your dataset is one plant at one hour. For example:

Plant: SOLAR_KA_01, Hour: 2024-01-15 09:00, actual generation: 42 MW
Plant: WIND_KA_07, Hour: 2024-01-15 09:00, actual generation: 18 MW

You have maybe 20 plants, each with 8760 hours in a year, so your dataset has around 175,200 rows.
For a specific plant, say SOLAR_KA_01, you want to predict: given the weather forecast and what we know about this plant's physical characteristics, how many MW will it produce at 14:00 tomorrow?
The model sees the weather features, the time of day, the plant's location and capacity, and outputs a number in MW. That is a prediction for that plant at that hour.

Why One Model for All Plants (the Global Model Design)
The naive approach would be: train one model for SOLAR_KA_01, another for SOLAR_KA_02, and so on. But each plant might only have one year of hourly data, which is around 8760 rows. That is not much to learn from.
Instead, you train one model on all plants together. Each row still represents one plant at one hour, but now the model has 175,200 rows total. The model knows which plant it is looking at because the feature vector includes the plant's physical identity: its capacity in MW, its latitude and longitude encoded as sin and cos, its tilt angle if solar, its hub height if wind, and its plant type encoded as 0 or 1.
So when the model sees a row with capacity_mw=50, lat_sin=0.24, lat_cos=0.97, plant_type_enc=0 (solar), it learns that this particular combination of numbers corresponds to a specific solar plant in northern Karnataka and behaves accordingly. The model does not need separate training per plant because the plant identity is baked into the features.

Stage 1 in Detail
Stage 1 is the global LightGBM model trained to answer: given all the features for a particular plant at a particular hour, what is the expected generation in MW?
What LightGBM is doing internally: it builds a sequence of decision trees, each one learning from the errors of the previous trees. It learns rules like "if CMF is above 0.8 and hour is between 10 and 14 and the plant is solar and capacity is 50 MW, predict around 40 MW." These rules get very specific and the model captures complex interactions between weather, time, and plant characteristics.
The 17 features it uses:
Physics outputs tell the model what the physics of the plant says should happen. CMF (Cloud Modification Factor) for solar tells you how much of the clear-sky output survives the cloud cover. Power curve fraction for wind tells you where on the turbine's power curve the current wind speed sits.
Weather features like temperature and NWP spread (the spread in the numerical weather prediction ensemble, which represents forecast uncertainty) give context beyond the physics outputs.
Asset metadata features are the plant identity: capacity, lat sin, lat cos, lon sin, lon cos, tilt angle, hub height. The sin and cos encoding of latitude and longitude is used because raw degrees are arbitrary numbers whereas sin and cos capture circular geography properly.
Time encoding features are hour sin, hour cos, day-of-year sin, day-of-year cos, and season. Again, sin and cos are used so that the model knows hour 23 is close to hour 0, and December is close to January, rather than treating them as numerically distant.
Plant type encoded is 0 for solar and 1 for wind, telling the model which physics regime it is in.

train_stage1(X_train, y_train, X_val, y_val)
This function creates a LightGBM regressor and calls .fit() on it. The training data is everything before the last 4 months. The validation data is the 2 months before the final test period.
Early stopping is set to 50 rounds. This means: if the model's performance on the validation set has not improved for 50 consecutive trees, stop adding more trees. This prevents overfitting. Without it, the model would keep adding trees until it memorises the training data and performs poorly on new data.
The model is returned as a Python object that can be called to make predictions.

predict_stage1(model, X)
Takes the trained model and a feature matrix X, calls .predict(), and returns an array of MW predictions, one per row. If X has 48 rows (2 plants, 24 hours each), you get back 48 numbers.

train_and_eval_stage1(train_df, val_df, test_df) in main.py
This is the orchestration function for Stage 1. It calls transform() from feature_engineering.py on each split to get the feature matrices. It trains the model on the training split with the validation split for early stopping. Then it runs predictions on the test split and prints the fleet-normalised MAE and RMSE.
Fleet-normalised means: instead of reporting the error in raw MW, you divide by the total capacity of all plants. So if total capacity is 500 MW and your mean absolute error is 25 MW, the nMAE is 0.05, meaning your forecast is off by 5% of the fleet's maximum output on average. This normalisation makes the metric meaningful regardless of how large or small the fleet is.

Stage 2 in Detail
Stage 2 is a second LightGBM model that is trained to predict the error that Stage 1 will make on future hours, using only information that is available at the time of prediction.
The key insight is this: if Stage 1 has been consistently underpredicting a plant all morning because an unexpected cloud bank arrived, that pattern of errors is itself informative. Stage 2 learns to look at recent errors and say "this plant has been running 8 MW below forecast for the last 4 hours, and based on the current CMF and hour of day, it is likely to continue running about 6 MW below forecast in the next few hours." Then you add that correction to the Stage 1 forecast for the afternoon.
Stage 2 is a corrector on top of Stage 1. It does not replace Stage 1. The final forecast for any remaining hour is always: Stage 1 prediction plus Stage 2 correction.

build_stage2_training_data(df_with_s1_pred)
This is the most complex function in the codebase. Its job is to generate a training dataset for Stage 2 by simulating every possible intra-day scenario that could occur during the training period.
The function receives a DataFrame that already has both the actual generation values and the Stage 1 predictions for every row. It also has the raw features like CMF, plant metadata, and timestamps.
For every combination of plant and date, it takes that one day's 24 hours of data sorted chronologically. Then it loops over every possible "split point" from hour 1 to hour 23. A split point of 6 means: imagine it is 06:00. You have actual readings for hours 0 through 5. You want to correct the Stage 1 forecast for hours 6 through 23.
For each split point, the function computes two summary statistics from the recent elapsed hours, specifically the last 6 or fewer hours that exist:
mean_recent_error is the average of (actual minus Stage 1 prediction) over those recent elapsed hours. If Stage 1 predicted 40 MW but the plant actually produced 32 MW, the error for that hour is minus 8 MW. If this pattern holds across several hours, the mean recent error will be strongly negative, signalling that Stage 1 is overforecasting.
std_recent_error is the standard deviation of those same recent errors. A low standard deviation means the errors are consistent and predictable. A high standard deviation means the errors are volatile and the correction should be less confident.
For every remaining hour in the day (hours after the split point), it creates one training row with those two residual summary statistics, plus the hour of day, the CMF or power curve fraction at that specific future hour, and the plant's asset features. The target variable for that row is the actual error at that future hour, meaning actual generation minus Stage 1 prediction.
After this loop runs across all plants and all days and all split points, you have a large dataset where each row says: "given that recent errors looked like this, and the physics signal at this future hour is this, and this is the plant type and location, what error will Stage 1 make?" Stage 2 learns to answer that question.
The reason this works is that intra-day error patterns are correlated. If a plant is running below forecast in the morning due to unexpected cloud cover, that cloud cover often persists for several hours. Stage 2 learns these persistence patterns.

train_stage2(X2_train, y2_train, X2_val, y2_val)
Identical structure to train_stage1. It trains another LightGBM regressor, this time on the Stage 2 dataset where the target is the Stage 1 error rather than the raw generation. The same early stopping logic applies.

predict_stage2(stage2_model, mean_recent_error, std_recent_error, hour_of_day, CMF, power_curve_fraction, capacity_mw, lat_sin, lat_cos, lon_sin, lon_cos)
This function is called once per remaining hour per plant at inference time. It takes all the Stage 2 features as individual arguments, assembles them into a single-row DataFrame, and calls the Stage 2 model's predict method. It returns a single scalar, the predicted correction in MW.
For example, if Stage 2 returns minus 6.3, that means "based on morning evidence, Stage 1 is likely to overpredict this plant by about 6.3 MW at this future hour." The final forecast becomes Stage 1 prediction minus 6.3.

intraday_update(stage1_model, stage2_model, day_df, cutoff_hour)
This is the top-level inference function for the intra-day use case. You call it when it is currently cutoff_hour and you want corrected forecasts for all remaining hours of the day.
It receives the full day's data for all plants, including actual values for elapsed hours (before cutoff) and features for all hours including future ones.
For each plant, it first runs the full 24-hour Stage 1 forecast by calling transform() on the day's features and then predict_stage1(). It then splits the day into elapsed rows (hour less than cutoff) and future rows (hour greater than or equal to cutoff).
From the elapsed rows, it computes mean and standard deviation of recent errors using the last 6 hours of Stage 1 errors, exactly as was done during Stage 2 training.
Then for every future hour, it calls predict_stage2() and computes the final prediction as s1_pred + correction.
The result is a DataFrame with one row per plant per future hour, showing the Stage 1 prediction, the correction amount, the corrected final prediction, and the actual value if known. This is what the grid operator would consume at, say, 12:00 noon to update their scheduling decisions for the afternoon.

eval_stage2_full(s1_model, s2_model, test_df) in main.py
This function validates that Stage 2 actually improves forecasts. It runs the intra-day simulation with cutoff_hour=12 for every plant and every day in the test set. It collects the Stage 1 predictions and Stage 2 corrected predictions for afternoon hours (12:00 onward) and computes the nMAE of each. Then it prints the improvement percentage.
If Stage 2 is working, you will see a positive improvement number, meaning the corrected forecasts are closer to actuals than the raw Stage 1 forecasts for the afternoon hours. If Stage 2 shows no improvement, the likely cause is that the recent error features computed during training were not truly informative, possibly because error patterns do not persist in the data.

prepare_stage2_data(s1_model, df_slice, label) in main.py
This is a helper that takes either the validation DataFrame or test DataFrame, runs Stage 1 predictions on it, attaches those predictions to the DataFrame alongside the actual values, and then calls build_stage2_training_data(). It exists to avoid repeating the same three lines twice in the training pipeline.
The reason Stage 2 is trained on val-set Stage 1 predictions rather than train-set predictions is important: on the training set, Stage 1 has already seen those rows during training and its "errors" would be unrealistically small. The validation set is data Stage 1 has never seen, so its errors on the validation set are representative of how it will err in the real world. Stage 2 must learn from realistic errors, not artificially small ones.

The Full Flow End to End
When you run main.py with a data file, it loads and splits the data temporally. It trains Stage 1 on the training split. It evaluates Stage 1 on the test split to get the baseline nMAE. It then generates Stage 2 training data from the validation split by running Stage 1 on it and computing errors. It trains Stage 2 on that residual dataset using the test split as its validation. It then runs the intra-day simulation for every test day at noon and reports whether Stage 2 improves upon Stage 1 for afternoon hours. Finally, it saves both models to disk so they can be loaded later for live inference when the system is deployed.
In deployment, the operator would load both saved models. At 06:00, they call intraday_update() with cutoff_hour=6 to get corrected forecasts for hours 6 through 23. At 12:00, they call it again with cutoff_hour=12 for the afternoon. The Stage 1 model provides the structural day-ahead forecast. Stage 2 adapts it to whatever the weather actually turned out to be that morning.
